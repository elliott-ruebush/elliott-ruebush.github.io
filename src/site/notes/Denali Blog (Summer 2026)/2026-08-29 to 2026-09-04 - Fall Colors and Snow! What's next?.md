### Change of Plans Hike
Originally, we were aiming to walk out to the newly open (to pedestrians and bikes) Pretty Rocks Bridge, but the bears have been active recently, and said activity meant the route was closed. 

Sidenote: the bears are kinda bullies, wolves will take down a caribou or other large animal, then the bears will just move in and chase them off the carcass after letting the wolves do the dirty work. This makes for an interesting dynamic between our apex predators (I assume they don't love running into each other).

Anyways, due to the bear closure, we backtracked a bit and chose a different area to hike. We went up a drainage, saw some cool rocks, got thoroughly rained on, and met the sickest lil' squad of gnar-shredding juvenile Harlequin ducks.

### Geology Hike in the Snow!
I made the last minute decision to join for a hike led by our park geologists. It was raining and just above freezing at park headquarters, but we made the call to go ahead with the hike and experienced beautiful fresh snow (snow and below freezing >>> rain and just above freezing) once we got a bit further up the road. 

I've never frolicked in inches of fresh snow in August before (this was August 31st), so that was a fun new experience! We did have a dusting of snow higher up in July as well (although no frolicking was done in that snowfall), so I think this year will mark my first time seeing snow all of the summer months! Question for the class - if it could snow at any point of the year, where do you draw the line between the last snow of spring and the first snow of fall?

#### Geology stuff!
Of course, part of the point of the hike was also to learn some about the park's geology. Among various tidbits, we learned that rhyolite (which I chronically mispronounce for some reason) is more incompetent (that's the technical term for weaker!) than basalt. This directly informed construction decisions around the bridge we were trying to see the other day. One side is built on firmer basalt, but the other side is built on less competent rhyolite. To shore up the weaker side, they're using thermosyphons that help keep the permafrost firm and frozen. That's a pretty cool (ha) piece of engineering!
![Pasted image 20260907070559.png](/img/user/Pasted%20image%2020260907070559.png)

Another fun geology item we saw was the sand gauge! It's a little plastic card to help field ID rocks that has different types of material glued onto it. 
I also learned that we draw nice boxes to classify rocks and give them distinct names, but in reality there's a continuum of their chemical composition that different types of rock could fall along. You can get different flavors of diorite that may be closer to a granite or a gabbro.

### The end of the season is coming!
The snow has made an appearance! Temps are getting cooler, colors are changing, and folks are starting to wrap their seasons up. It feels like summer is well on its way out up in Alaska, which is an interesting juxtaposition against the warmth that we still have back down in the lower 48. Everything is condensed down into a rapid-fire few months of summer up in the land of the midnight sun!
